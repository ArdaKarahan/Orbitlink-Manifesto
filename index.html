Orbitlink Manifesto

Once upon a time, when only governments had the resources to develop space technology, the humanity agreed on the Space Treaty in 1967. This treaty includes the following:

  • The exploration and use of outer space shall be carried out for the benefit and in the interests of all countries and shall be the province of all mankind;
  • Outer space shall be free for exploration and use by all States;
  • States shall be responsible for national space activities whether carried out by governmental or non-governmental entities;

The treaty, by all means, states that outer space is open to the usage of all humanity, and it is our common heritage. Although the treaty had a good intent and was severely important for humanity's progression at the time, it came with a side effect that does not align with today's and future's capabilities: The control of the space being in the hands of states.

Mankind is moving towards an era that allows regular people to interact with space technologies, not just elites or states. As satellites providing emergency services, messaging, and even internet today, the audience of the space technologies evolved to become the whole humanity.

As of today, humanity can provide free access to data all around the globe, regardless of one's location. The only moat for this kind of service is not technical, but political. There are still states who restrict the communication of their own people and isolating them from the rest of the world. They silence voices, they oppress crowds. These restrictive states are an ultimate form of shameless materialism. They treat people as statistics, and they would exceed every law of ethics to maintain authority. As Orbitlink, we claim humanity's right to access and transfer data is non-negotiable, and no political interest of any state can justify neglecting this right.

From the moment humanity settled to an area, built cities, and formed communities, it has found ways to express. Thoughts, feelings, information… We have formed languages, burnt fires, trained pigeons, and covered the earth with wires. Communication is what made us, for the way to community is to communicate. The technologies of the past did not allow for such global and censorship-resistant data access, but space technologies do. Now that circumstances are convenient for such advancement, we should not let any central power to abuse this fundamental human right.

The communication technologies are so important for humanity that no state or institution should hold the power to deny it. Orbitlink states that The Outer Space Treaty does not meet the needs of humanity today, for the users of space technologies are no longer just states but the whole humanity. It should also be mentioned that we are not simple anarchists that oppose any type of law. We believe the law must serve fellow humans, and the law that can't must be either abolished or regulated. Law must allow any provider to provide data access to any point in the world without governments interrupting them to do so.

We also believe that providers of such communication services should not have any authority over the user data. Providers should not be able to see or manipulate the data they relay.

"When I purchase a magazine at a store and hand cash to the clerk, there is no need to know who I am. When I ask my electronic mail provider to send and receive messages, my provider need not know to whom I am speaking or what I am saying or what others are saying to me; my provider only need know how to get the message there and how much I owe them in fees."
We believe providers should only provide communication, not control it. Physics, mathematics and cryptography must control such systems.

Orbitlink writes code. We aim to build systems that will allow for decentralized data relay from any point of the world, to any another point of the world. Our code is also free to use, worldwide; and we also know that a widely dispersed system can't be shut down. People around the world must work together for such freedom and privacy of their own data.

Whether it's a text, a voice or a call
Space belongs to us all.
Onward.

Arda Karahan
